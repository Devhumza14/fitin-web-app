Designing a whole home is a big project with many moving parts: layouts, joinery, lighting, furniture, finishes and the people who deliver each of them. Most of the stress comes from doing things in the wrong order, or from decisions that were never really made.

This checklist breaks a full-home project into stages. You can use it whether you are building a new house, fitting out an apartment or giving an older home a complete refresh.

## Before you start

- **Decide who lives in the home and how.** Who needs a desk, who needs storage, who has mobility needs, and how often do you host guests?
- **Collect the paperwork.** Floor plans, any approvals, and the details of any building rules that limit changes.
- **Walk the space together.** Note what you love, what annoys you, and what must not change.
- **Gather references.** A handful of photos you like is more useful than a long description. Notice what they have in common.

## Write the brief and set the budget

A brief does not need to be long. One page covering the rooms, the style you are drawn to, the must-haves and the things you want to avoid is enough to start a good conversation.

Set the budget before you see designs. Include furniture, lighting and soft furnishings, not only the building work, and keep a reserve for the unexpected. A designer can tell you where money is best spent, but only if they know the limit. ## The design phase

1. **Site visit and measurements.** Your designer measures every room and notes the position of services.
2. **Layouts.** Furniture and joinery positions are planned room by room, then checked against how you actually move around.
3. **Concept and materials.** You see a material board with finishes, colours and key pieces. Ask to see real samples, not only photos.
4. **Drawings and quotes.** Each element should be drawn and quoted as separate lines, so you can compare and adjust.
5. **Approval.** Sign off on the plan in writing before production begins. Changes after this point cost time and money.

## The build phase

Work usually follows an order that avoids damaging finished surfaces:

- Structural and layout changes first.
- Electrical, plumbing and air-conditioning work next.
- Ceilings, then wall finishes and flooring.
- Joinery such as kitchens and wardrobes once surfaces are ready.
- Painting and final touches.

Ask your designer for a written schedule and check progress against it. One person should be responsible for coordinating the trades, so you are not managing each of them separately.

## Furniture, lighting and the final fit

Furniture with long lead times is best ordered early, even if it will not arrive until the end. Lighting plans should be settled before the ceilings are closed. When items arrive, check them against the approved drawings and inspect for damage before the delivery team leaves.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting without a budget for furniture.** Building work often uses up the money, leaving the rooms bare. Plan the full cost, including furniture and lighting, from the start.
- **Choosing finishes room by room.** Each room can look fine alone and still clash in the hallway. Look at the whole home on one material board.
- **Changing the plan mid-build.** Moving a socket on paper costs nothing. Moving it after the wall is finished costs time and money.
- **Ignoring storage.** A beautiful home with nowhere to put things quickly becomes cluttered. Count what you own and plan storage for it.
- **Skipping the written schedule.** Without dates, small delays pile up and nobody is accountable.

## Questions to ask your designer

- Who will be on site, and how often will they visit?
- How are changes priced and approved?
- What is included in the quote, and what is not?
- How will you keep me updated on progress?
- What happens if something arrives damaged or late?

Clear answers to these questions at the start save a lot of argument later.

## Handover

Walk through every room with your designer and list any defects. Check that doors, drawers and hinges work, that switches control the right lights, and that finishes match the samples you approved. Agree a date for the corrections and ask for care instructions for the materials used.

## Common questions

**How long does a full-home project take?** It depends on the size of the home, the amount of building work and the lead time of the items you choose. Ask your designer for a written schedule with dates for each stage.

**Can I live in the home during the work?** Sometimes, for lighter projects. Heavy building work is easier and quicker if the home is empty.

**Do I have to decide everything at once?** No, but major choices such as layouts, joinery and lighting should be settled before building starts. Decorative items can follow later.

**What if I change my mind?** Tell your designer as early as possible. A change before production is easy to absorb. After production starts it usually costs more and delays the schedule.

## Keep one source of truth

Many problems come from information scattered across chat messages and phone calls. Whatever system you use, keep the drawings, quotes, approvals and schedule in one place that everyone can see.
