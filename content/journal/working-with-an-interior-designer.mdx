Hiring an interior designer is not the same as handing over your home and waiting. The best results come from a clear working relationship, where you know what to give, what to expect and when to speak up.

This guide covers each stage of working with a designer, so you can get a home that feels like yours.

## What a designer does

A good interior designer does more than choose colours. They:

- Measure the space and understand how you live in it.
- Plan layouts that make the rooms work.
- Select materials, finishes, furniture and lighting.
- Prepare drawings and quotes so the work can be built correctly.
- Coordinate with vendors and tradespeople.
- Check the work as it is built and at handover.

Knowing the scope helps you ask the right questions and avoid assuming that something is included when it is not.

## Prepare your brief

The more clearly you describe what you need, the better the first proposal will be. Prepare:

- **Who lives in the home:** ages, habits, working from home, pets.
- **What each room is for:** and what is not working today.
- **Must-haves and deal-breakers:** a prayer space, extra storage, space for guests.
- **Style references:** photos you like, and photos you dislike. Both are useful.
- **Budget:** a realistic figure, including furniture and a reserve.
- **Timeline:** any dates that matter, such as a wedding or a move.

Be honest about the budget. A designer cannot make good decisions about materials without it, and being open early avoids disappointment later.

## Review the concepts

Early on you will usually see a concept: layouts, a material board and key pieces. Take your time with it.

- Look at it in the context of your daily routine. Where will you put your shoes, your bags, your laptop?
- Ask to see real material samples in your own light.
- Ask what each choice costs, and where the designer has made a compromise.
- Take a day to think before you respond. First reactions are often not the final ones.

## Give useful feedback

Vague comments like "I don't love it" are hard to act on. Specific feedback gets results.

- **Say what you like first.** It tells the designer what to keep.
- **Describe the problem, not the solution.** "The sofa area feels cramped" helps more than "make the sofa smaller", because the designer may find a better answer.
- **Point to references.** "I like how the light feels in this photo" is clearer than adjectives.
- **Gather opinions before you reply.** If family members will have a say, collect their views first so the designer is not receiving conflicting instructions.

## Handle changes and approvals

Changes are normal, but they should be managed.

- **Agree the process early.** How many rounds of revisions are included, and what happens after approval?
- **Get approvals in writing.** A short message confirming the drawings or the material choice is enough.
- **Ask about the cost of changes before you make them.** A small change in the design phase is cheap. The same change after production has started can be costly.
- **Keep decisions in one place.** A single shared folder or thread avoids confusion.

## Agree how you will communicate

A project goes more smoothly when everyone knows how to reach each other.

- Pick one main channel for decisions, such as a shared chat or email thread, and keep it there.
- Agree how quickly each side will respond to questions.
- Decide who has the final say if the household disagrees.
- Ask for a short written summary after each meeting, listing what was agreed.

## Be realistic about time

Good design takes time at each stage: measuring, planning, reviewing, ordering and building. Rushing the early stages usually creates delays later, when mistakes show up on site. Ask your designer for a realistic schedule, and tell them early about any fixed dates.

## Trust, but check

Trust the designer's experience, especially on technical matters such as layouts, services and materials. At the same time, visit the site when you can, ask questions when something is unclear, and check that the work matches the approved plans. A good designer welcomes this.

## Common questions

**Do I need to know my style before I start?** No. Bring photos of rooms you like and dislike, and your designer will help you find the common thread.

**What if I do not like the first concept?** That is normal and expected. Explain what is not working, and the designer will revise it. Early revisions are far cheaper than changes during the build.

**Can I choose some items myself?** Often yes. Tell your designer early about pieces you already own or want to buy, so they can plan around them.

**How involved should I be during the build?** Visit at key points, such as after services are laid and before finishes go on, and keep decisions flowing so the work does not wait for you.
