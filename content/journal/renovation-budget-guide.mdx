Renovation budgets go wrong in a predictable way. The visible items, such as new flooring or a new kitchen, get priced carefully. The things that sit behind the walls, and the small items nobody thought of, do not. The result is a project that costs more than planned and runs longer than promised.

A good budget does not remove uncertainty, because old buildings always hide something. What it does is make room for that uncertainty so it does not become a crisis.

## What a renovation budget includes

Write the budget as a list of lines, not one number.

- **Design and planning:** drawings, site visits and any approvals.
- **Demolition and clearing:** removing old finishes, fittings and debris.
- **Structural and layout changes:** moving or removing walls, new openings.
- **Services:** electrical, plumbing, gas and air-conditioning work.
- **Surfaces:** flooring, ceilings, wall finishes and paint.
- **Joinery:** kitchens, wardrobes, built-in storage.
- **Fittings and fixtures:** sanitary ware, taps, lights, switches.
- **Furniture and soft furnishings:** often forgotten until the end.
- **Temporary costs:** storage, alternative accommodation, and cleaning at the end.

If a quote does not show a line for one of these, ask whether it is included, excluded or simply forgotten.

## Add a contingency

Open up an old wall or floor and you may find damp, outdated wiring or pipes that need replacing. Keep a reserve aside for this. A common approach is to hold back a sensible percentage of the total, and to decide with your designer what size suits the age and condition of your property. The older the building and the more you are changing, the larger the reserve should be.

Do not treat the contingency as spare money to spend on upgrades. Its job is to absorb surprises.

## Know the order of works

Doing work in the wrong order is expensive, because finished surfaces get damaged and redone. As a general sequence:

1. Planning, drawings and approvals.
2. Demolition and structural changes.
3. Services behind walls and above ceilings.
4. Ceilings, wall finishes and flooring.
5. Joinery and fittings.
6. Painting, lighting and final touches.
7. Furniture and styling.

A clear written schedule helps everyone see how a delay in one stage affects the next.

## Get comparable quotes

Three quotes are only useful if they describe the same work. Give each contractor the same drawings and the same list of materials, and ask for itemised prices. Then compare line by line.

Look at:

- **Scope:** what is included and what is not.
- **Materials:** brands, grades and finishes, not just "good quality".
- **Timeline:** start date, duration and what happens if work overruns.
- **Payment schedule:** payments should follow completed stages, not be taken mostly upfront.
- **Warranty and after-care:** what is covered and for how long.

The cheapest quote is often the one that has left things out.

## Red flags in a quote

- A single lump-sum figure with no breakdown.
- Vague descriptions such as "as required" or "standard finish".
- Pressure to pay a large share before work begins.
- No written schedule.
- Reluctance to put changes and extras in writing.

## Think about living through the work

Many people stay in the home during a renovation, and it changes daily life more than they expect. Dust, noise, water shut-offs and blocked rooms all become part of the routine.

- **Decide whether to move out**, fully or partly, for the noisiest stages.
- **Protect what stays.** Cover furniture and seal doorways to limit dust.
- **Plan essentials.** If the kitchen or a bathroom is out of action, arrange alternatives before work begins.
- **Set working hours** with neighbours and the building in mind.

These practical costs and inconveniences belong in the budget and the schedule too.

## Keep the plan stable

Every change after work starts affects cost and time. Before demolition begins, settle the layout, the fittings and the finishes. Visit showrooms, hold physical samples, and confirm what will be ordered. If you do make a change later, ask for the extra cost and time in writing before you agree.

## Common questions

**Should I pay the contractor upfront?** A small advance to start is common, but the rest should follow completed stages. Agree the schedule in writing before you pay anything.

**Is it cheaper to buy materials myself?** Sometimes, but it makes you responsible for quantities, delivery and defects. If you do buy them yourself, agree with your contractor who is responsible for what.

**How do I handle an extra that comes up mid-project?** Ask for the cost and the time impact in writing, decide, and record the decision. Do not agree to work verbally.

**What if the quotes are very different?** Compare scope line by line. A big gap usually means a missing item, a different material or a different standard of finish.

## Keep records

Keep every quote, drawing, approval and receipt together. When an extra comes up mid-project, ask for it in writing with the price before you agree. This protects you and the contractor.
