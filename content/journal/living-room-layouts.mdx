Most people plan a living room by choosing a sofa and working outwards. It is better to work the other way round: decide how the room will be used, then choose the furniture that supports it.

A living room often has to do many jobs. It hosts guests, serves as the family's evening gathering place, holds the television, and sometimes doubles as a prayer area, a play area or a work spot. A layout that reflects this makes the room comfortable every day.

## Start from use, not furniture

Write down what happens in the room on a normal week and on a busy day.

- Who sits where in the evening?
- How many people do you host, and how often?
- Do children play here? Does anyone work or study here?
- Is the room used for prayer, or for gatherings that need a lot of floor space?
- Does the room need to double as a guest bedroom?

The answers show what the room needs: flexible seating, more storage, a clear floor area, or a quiet corner.

## Create conversation zones

Seating works best when people can talk without raising their voices. Arrange the main pieces so that they face each other, rather than lining them against the walls.

- **Keep seating close enough** that people can speak comfortably, but not so close that knees touch.
- **Angle chairs towards the sofa** to form a loose circle or L-shape.
- **Anchor the group with a rug** large enough that the front legs of each piece sit on it.
- **Give each seat a surface** nearby, such as a side table or a coffee table within reach.

In a large room, you can form two zones, one for family and one for guests, with a console or a bookshelf as a soft divider.

## Plan the TV and media wall

A television changes the layout, so decide on its position early.

- **Height:** the centre of the screen should be near eye level when seated.
- **Distance:** sit far enough away that the screen is comfortable to watch.
- **Glare:** avoid placing the TV opposite a window, or use curtains that control the light.
- **Wiring:** plan power, network and cable routes before walls are finished, so no wires show.
- **Storage:** a media unit can hold consoles, speakers and papers behind closed doors.

A media wall can also be a design feature, with panelling, shelves or lighting that frames the screen rather than leaving it as a black rectangle.

## Keep walkways clear

People should be able to move through the room without squeezing between furniture. Leave a clear path from the entrance to each seat, and from the seating to other doors. Check how doors open, and make sure furniture does not block them.

If the room is narrow, choose slimmer furniture and arrange it along the length. If the room is open to a dining area, use rugs and the back of the sofa to mark where one space ends and the other begins.

## Layer the lighting

One ceiling light rarely makes a living room comfortable. Use several layers:

- **General light** from ceiling fixtures or cove lighting.
- **Task light** from a lamp beside a reading chair.
- **Accent light** to highlight art, shelves or a feature wall.

Put different layers on separate switches or dimmers. This lets you set a bright mood for guests and a soft one for an evening at home.

## Choose furniture that suits your household

Deep, soft sofas are comfortable, but older family members may find low seats hard to get out of. Light-coloured fabrics look fresh but show marks. Wooden frames and hard-wearing upholstery cope better with children and heavy use. Your designer can suggest options that look good and last.

## Small and large rooms

**In a small living room**, pick fewer, smaller pieces and keep the walkways clear. Choose a sofa with slim arms, use a nesting coffee table, and add storage with a slim console or built-in shelves. Light colours and a large mirror help the room feel more open.

**In a large living room**, avoid pushing everything against the walls, which leaves the middle empty. Pull seating into groups, use rugs to define each zone, and add lighting at several heights so the room does not feel cold.

## Think about prayer, play and guests

If the room doubles as a prayer space, leave a clear floor area and a place to store mats. If children play there, keep sharp corners and fragile items out of reach and choose hard-wearing fabrics. If you host often, add flexible seating such as ottomans or lightweight chairs that can be moved easily when guests arrive.

## Common questions

**Should the sofa face the TV?** Not necessarily. Many rooms work better with seating angled towards each other and the TV on a nearby wall, so conversation comes first.

**How big should the rug be?** Large enough that at least the front legs of the main seating sit on it. A rug that is too small makes the arrangement look disconnected.

**How many seats do I need?** Count your household and your regular guests, then add flexible extras such as ottomans or stools that can be tucked away.

**Can I mix furniture styles?** Yes, if you keep a common thread, such as a colour, a wood tone or a shape. A designer can help you tie pieces together.
