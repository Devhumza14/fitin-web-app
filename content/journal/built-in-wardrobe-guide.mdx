A built-in wardrobe is one of the most useful things you can add to a bedroom. It uses the full height of the wall, fits around the room's quirks, and keeps clothes organised in a way a loose wardrobe rarely does.

It is also easy to get wrong. A few decisions at the start, about doors, depth and layout, decide whether the wardrobe is a pleasure or a daily annoyance.

## Hinged or sliding doors

**Hinged doors** open fully, so you can see and reach everything inside at once. They need clear floor space in front, equal to the width of the door, so check the room before you commit. Narrower doors are easier on tight rooms than a few very wide ones.

**Sliding doors** do not swing out, which suits small bedrooms and wardrobes close to the bed. The trade-off is that you can only open part of the wardrobe at a time, and the track needs to be good quality so doors run smoothly for years.

A mix is possible too: sliding doors for the main section, hinged doors for tall sections or special storage.

## Depth matters

Depth is easy to overlook, and it affects how comfortable the wardrobe is to use. Clothes on hangers need a minimum depth to hang freely without pressing against the doors. If the wardrobe is too shallow, clothes get crushed. If it is too deep, items at the back get lost.

Your designer will suggest a depth based on how you plan to use each section, and on how much floor space the room can spare. Measure the room carefully, including skirting boards and any wall that is not perfectly straight.

## Hanging versus drawers

Decide what you own before you decide the layout.

- **Long hanging:** for dresses, coats, abayas, sherwanis and long kurtas.
- **Short hanging:** for shirts and trousers, which leaves space below for drawers or shoes.
- **Drawers:** for folded clothes, underwear and accessories. Deep drawers are easier to use than shelves.
- **Shelves:** for items you want to see at a glance, such as bags or folded sweaters.
- **Shoe storage:** pull-out racks or angled shelves, planned near the base.

Count your clothes by type, and ask yourself what you reach for most. Place those items at a comfortable height, and use the highest shelves for items you rarely need, such as luggage or occasional bedding.

## Materials and humidity

Wardrobe materials need to cope with your climate. In humid cities, moisture can damage poor-quality board and make doors swell or warp. Ask about moisture-resistant board for the carcass, and good edge sealing so water cannot get into the core.

Door finishes range from laminates to lacquers and veneers. Think about how they will look after years of use, how easy they are to clean, and whether the colour will fade in direct sunlight.

Hardware deserves attention too. Good hinges, drawer runners and sliding tracks make the difference between a wardrobe that feels solid and one that rattles. They are small items, but you use them every day.

## Lighting inside

Light inside the wardrobe makes a big difference, especially in deep sections. Strip lights that switch on when the door opens help you find things quickly and show true colours. Plan the wiring early, because it is much harder to add once the unit is installed.

## Think about how you get dressed

The best wardrobe fits your routine. Notice how you get dressed each day.

- Do you choose clothes the night before, or in a rush in the morning?
- Do two people share the wardrobe? If so, give each person a clearly separate section.
- Do you need a place to lay out an outfit, or a mirror inside the door?
- Do you store seasonal or occasion wear that you use only a few times a year?

Designing around these habits makes the wardrobe easier to keep tidy.

## Accessories and details

Small fittings turn a plain wardrobe into a useful one: pull-out trouser racks, tie and belt organisers, jewellery trays with soft lining, laundry baskets and valet rods. Ask your designer which of these suit what you own. They can be planned from the start, which is easier than adding them later.

## Walk-in or fitted?

If you have the space, a walk-in wardrobe gives the most flexibility, but it uses a lot of floor area. A well-planned fitted wardrobe often stores just as much in a smaller footprint. Your designer can compare both against your room.

## Common questions

**How tall should a wardrobe be?** Many built-in wardrobes run to the ceiling, which gives the most storage. Use the top section for rarely used items, with a step stool for access.

**Can a wardrobe be moved later?** Built-in units are fixed to the room, so plan them well. Modular designs can be adjusted or extended more easily than a one-off carpentry piece.

**What about mirrors?** A full-length mirror inside a door or on the end of the unit saves wall space and helps with dressing.

**Do I need to plan around the AC?** Yes. Check where split units, vents and pipes run, and keep the wardrobe clear of them.
