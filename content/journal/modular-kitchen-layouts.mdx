The layout is the most important decision in a modular kitchen, and the hardest one to change later. Cabinets, counters and hardware can be swapped or upgraded, but once the plumbing, gas and electrical points are in place, the layout is effectively fixed.

Cooking in many Pakistani homes is also different from the examples in most design magazines. Daily cooking is often heavy, with long hours at the hob, lots of frying and tempering, large pots, and several people in and out of the kitchen. A good layout starts from those habits, not from a picture.

This guide walks through the common layouts, when each one fits, and the practical details that decide whether a kitchen is pleasant or frustrating to use.

## Start with the work triangle

Designers still use the idea of a work triangle: the sink, the hob and the fridge are the three points you move between most. If they are too far apart you walk more than you need to. If they are too close, there is nowhere to put a chopping board.

A useful rule of thumb is to keep each leg of the triangle comfortable to walk, and to keep traffic from other rooms from crossing through it. If the main door to the kitchen opens right between the sink and the hob, people will constantly cross your path while you cook.

## Which layout fits which space

### Galley (parallel runs)
Two parallel runs of cabinets with a walkway between them. This is the most efficient use of a narrow room and works well when the kitchen has doors at both ends. The walkway needs to be wide enough for two open drawers or doors without blocking each other.

### L-shape
Cabinets along two adjacent walls. It suits square or slightly rectangular rooms and leaves the other side open for a small dining table. The corner needs thought: a blind corner wastes space unless it has a pull-out or carousel unit.

### U-shape
Cabinets along three walls. It gives the most counter and storage and keeps everything within a few steps. It needs enough width that the centre of the "U" does not feel like a corridor, so measure before you commit.

### Island or peninsula
An island adds a prep surface and seating, and it is popular for open kitchens. It only works if there is room to walk around it comfortably on every side. In a smaller space, a peninsula attached to one run gives most of the benefit without taking up the middle of the room.

### Single run
All cabinets along one wall. It is the simplest option for very small kitchens, studio flats or an open-plan space where the kitchen is one wall of a larger room.

## Plan storage around daily cooking

Think about what you actually reach for every day and put it within easy reach.

- **Near the hob:** oils, spices, tempering tools, lids and the pots you use most.
- **Near the sink:** the dish rack, cleaning supplies and bins, ideally behind a door or inside a pull-out.
- **Near the prep zone:** knives, chopping boards, mixing bowls and the appliances you use for prep.
- **High and low:** keep the heavy items low, the rarely used items high.

Deep drawers usually work better than shelves behind doors for pots and pans, because you can see and reach everything at once.

## Ventilation and the hood

Heavy cooking produces smoke, steam and grease, so ventilation matters more than most people expect. A hood needs to be sized for the hob underneath it and positioned at a sensible height above it. Whether it vents to the outside or recirculates depends on your building, and that is something to settle with your designer before the cabinets are made, because it changes where ducting and wall cabinets can go.

Also consider where the hob sits relative to windows and doors. A hob directly in a cross-draught can make flames flicker and make the hood less effective.

## Measure your wall properly

Before you talk to anyone, measure the length of each wall where cabinets could go, note the position of windows, doors, plumbing and gas, and measure the ceiling height. Take a few photos. Mark any beams or columns that cannot be moved.

## Bringing it together

There is no single best layout. The best one is the one that fits your room, your cooking habits and the number of people who use the kitchen. A designer will usually sketch two or three options on your actual measurements, so you can compare them before anything is built.
